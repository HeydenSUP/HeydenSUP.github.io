A COUNTER INVESTIGATION

Who Belongs? investigates Chinese migration, exclusion and belonging in Victoria, beginning with the Gold Rush of the 1850s and extending into the present.

Through photography, historical research, Dada-inspired photomontage and public distribution, the project asks how Chinese experiences fit within the familiar story of the Victorian Gold Rush.

What changed? What remained? Who belongs?

01
GOLD → MIGRATION

GOLD
CHANGED
VICTORIA.

Gold discoveries in Victoria in 1851 transformed the colony and drew migrants from across the world.

Among them were thousands of Chinese migrants. The Australian goldfields became known as Xin Jin Shan, or “New Gold Mountain”, distinguishing them from California’s earlier “Old Gold Mountain”.

By 1859, more than 40,000 Chinese migrants were living in Victoria. State Library Victoria estimates that Chinese miners represented roughly one in five men on the Victorian goldfields.

GOLD = OPPORTUNITY?
MIGRATION WAS NOT ONLY ARRIVAL.

It involved long-distance travel, debt, uncertainty and the hope of sending money back to families in China.
02
NEW GOLD MOUNTAIN

MORE THAN
MINERS.

Chinese migrants became part of the economic, social and cultural life of colonial Victoria.

ON THE GOLDFIELDS

In 1861, more than 24,000 Chinese immigrants were living on Victorian goldfields including Ballarat, Bendigo, Ararat, Beechworth, Castlemaine and Maryborough.

Many worked alluvial deposits using techniques such as panning, sluicing and puddling. But Chinese life in Victoria was never limited to mining alone.

COMMUNITY

Chinese settlements included shops, restaurants, temples, doctors, letter writers and other services.

These communities created social networks and cultural institutions while adapting to the conditions of colonial Victoria.

NOT ONLY MINERS
TRADERS
GARDENERS
DOCTORS
CRAFTSPEOPLE
SHOPKEEPERS
COMMUNITY BUILDERS

As gold deposits declined, many Chinese Victorians moved into market gardening, farming, grocery businesses, import and export, laundries, furniture making and medicine.

By 1861, Chinese people made up nearly seven per cent of Victoria’s population. Little Bourke Street developed into a major Chinese cultural and commercial centre.

03
FIELD INVESTIGATION

BALLARAT
AS PUBLIC MEMORY

Ballarat is not only a place where the Gold Rush happened. It is also a place where the Gold Rush is continually reconstructed, displayed and remembered.

SOVEREIGN HILL

Sovereign Hill is a living museum built on the Ballarat goldfields. Streets, mines, shops, workshops, schools and costumed interpretation recreate elements of nineteenth-century life.

During our field investigation, we photographed mining spaces, horse-drawn transport, historic trades and gold panning. These photographs became material for our Dada photomontage.

CHINESE PROTECTORATE CAMP

Chinese miners were also an important part of Ballarat’s goldfields.

The colonial government created a protectorate system that regulated aspects of Chinese life on the goldfields. Sovereign Hill now interprets this history through its reconstructed Chinese Protectorate Camp.

The camp was completely rebuilt in 2024, adding new buildings and expanded interpretation.

04
WHEN MIGRATION BECAME A “PROBLEM”

FROM COLONIAL
EXCLUSION TO
WHITE AUSTRALIA.

The promise of the Gold Rush did not operate equally for everyone.

As Chinese migration increased during the 1850s, racial prejudice, cultural difference and economic competition contributed to growing hostility. Colonial governments responded with laws specifically targeting Chinese migration.

1855
COLONIAL RESTRICTION

Chinese Immigration Act

Victoria introduced legislation specifically restricting Chinese immigration during the Gold Rush.

This was a colonial policy. Australia had not yet federated, and each colony still controlled its own migration laws.

1901
FEDERATION

Immigration Restriction Act

After Federation, the new Commonwealth Parliament created a national system of immigration restriction.

The Immigration Restriction Act 1901 became a cornerstone of the unofficial policy commonly known as the White Australia policy.

WHITE AUSTRALIA WAS NOT THE OFFICIAL NAME OF A SINGLE LAW.

The term describes a broader system of laws, policies and administrative practices intended to limit non-white immigration and maintain Australia as a predominantly white British society.

50
WORDS

The Dictation Test

Immigration officers could require a person to write 50 dictated words in a European language.

The language could be chosen so that the applicant was likely to fail, allowing officials to exclude people regarded as “undesirable”.

ARRIVE PASS LANGUAGE EXCLUDE TEST ENTER FIFTY WORDS
1958
DISMANTLING

Migration Act

The Migration Act 1958 ended the Immigration Restriction Act and abolished the dictation test.

1975
RACIAL DISCRIMINATION ACT

The remaining legal traces

The Racial Discrimination Act 1975 made racial discrimination unlawful and removed the remaining legal traces of the White Australia policy.

THE LAW CHANGED.
BUT DID BELONGING CHANGE WITH IT?
05
OUR RESPONSE

THREE
QUESTIONS.

01

WHAT CHANGED?

THE LAW.

Laws that explicitly restricted immigration on racial grounds were gradually dismantled.

Chinese communities also became increasingly established across Victoria, contributing to business, agriculture, culture, education and everyday life.

02

WHAT REMAINED?

THE QUESTION OF MEMORY.

Popular images of the Gold Rush still often centre gold, adventure, opportunity and prosperity.

Our investigation asks how that story changes when Chinese migration, discriminatory legislation and community life are placed back into it.

03

WHO BELONGS?

BELONGING IS MORE THAN ARRIVAL.

Chinese migrants were present on the Victorian goldfields from the early years of the Gold Rush.

But being physically present does not automatically mean being equally represented in public memory.

“Who belongs?” is therefore not only a question about immigration. It is also a question about whose experiences become part of the stories Australia tells about itself.

CONSTRUCTED PUBLIC RESPONSE
WHEN YOU THINK ABOUT THE VICTORIAN GOLD RUSH, WHAT COMES TO MIND?
“Gold. Miners. Opportunity.”
“I DON’T REMEMBER LEARNING MUCH ABOUT CHINESE MINERS.”

FROM DIGITAL TO PHYSICAL

TAKE
THE
STORY.

The project extends beyond the website through a printed A6 postcard.

The postcard transforms our Dada-inspired photomontage into a small physical object that can be freely taken, read and carried beyond its original display space.

Rather than keeping the investigation only online, the postcard allows the counter-narrative to circulate through a public cultural space.
